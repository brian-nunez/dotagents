# Visual direction

Read this once the task is understood and the design needs a coherent character, or when Brian requests a different look and feel.

## Translate taste into decisions

Translate intended qualities into visible hypotheses:

| Desired quality | Decisions to explore | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calm and focused | Restrained palette, clear grouping, modest elevation, quiet supporting text | Essential actions becoming hard to discover |
| Precise and capable | Strong alignment, consistent numbers, deliberate density, explicit states | Tiny type and an intimidating wall of data |
| Warm and approachable | Human copy, warmer neutrals, welcoming imagery, softer shapes | Decorative clutter or loss of seriousness |
| Editorial and expressive | Distinctive display type, intentional composition, content-led imagery | Display treatments impairing body-text legibility |
| Premium and restrained | Controlled spacing, materials, typography, and imagery | Empty space replacing useful information or generic luxury tropes |

These are directions to evaluate, not psychological facts about fonts or colors. A serif does not automatically establish trust; blue does not automatically establish security.

## Use references analytically

For each supplied reference, identify what is worth carrying forward: rhythm, reading measure, density, contrast distribution, image treatment, navigation, interaction, or copy tone. Ask what Brian dislikes as well as what he likes.

Extract principles rather than copying a competitor's exact layout. Preserve an established product identity unless the brief authorizes changing it. Do not introduce an unrelated trend merely to make a design appear contemporary.

If additional reference browsing would materially help, keep the search bounded by a question, such as comparing ways to present a complex selection. Browsing is not a substitute for interviewing Brian.

## Explore meaningful alternatives

When direction is uncertain, use a small number of distinct concepts. Vary decisions that matter: density, type voice, composition, image use, or navigation emphasis. Changing only a button color does not test a visual direction.

Use the same realistic content and primary task across concepts so they can be compared fairly. Explain which audience/task each serves and its main compromise. Recommend one with reasons, while preserving Brian's decision.

If Brian specified a direction, develop it. Avoid creating multiple concepts as a ritual. If uncertainty concerns one component, explore that component rather than several unrelated full pages.

## Establish a small visual vocabulary

Choose initial roles rather than an exhaustive catalog:

- Type roles for page context, section labels, main values, body copy, metadata, and controls.
- Spacing steps with distinct purposes, from related items through section separation.
- Neutral surfaces and foregrounds; a primary accent; necessary semantic states.
- Control shapes and emphasis levels; a consistent approach to separators and elevation.
- Image and icon treatments suited to the content and display size.

Choose enough variation to express hierarchy without creating a new style for every element. Different roles may justify different radii or widths; consistency means related decisions behave predictably, not that every element is identical.

## Refine in the right order

1. Make the primary content and task work at low fidelity.
2. Check grouping and emphasis without relying on accent color.
3. Apply typography, color roles, surfaces, and selected imagery.
4. Rebalance the complete page; a locally beautiful component may overpower its context.
5. Add finishing details where they reinforce character, meaning, or affordance.

A grayscale check is diagnostic, not a mandate to deliver monochrome work. A deliberately expressive design can use strong color once hierarchy supports it.

Give existing elements character before adding new ones: a considered quotation mark, list marker, selected state, or section accent may supply enough distinction. Explain decoration through the brief rather than requiring every page to have gradients, cards, or illustrations.

## Review criteria

- The direction is described through actual decisions rather than adjectives alone.
- The primary task remains clear when decoration is ignored.
- Typography, color, shape, imagery, and tone support the same experience.
- Reference influence is selective and suited to this audience.
- Brian can identify which direction is proposed and why.
